# Inventory UI and workflow audit

Reviewed 27 September 2026 against the current checkout and REQUIREMENTS_ARCHITECTURE.md.

## Implementation follow-up — 28 September 2026

This audit is the **pre-implementation baseline**. Its original screen observations and findings are retained for traceability. The subsequent implementation covers P0 F01–F09 and backend-available P1 F10–F14/F18 from the [feature backlog](FEATURE_GAP_ANALYSIS.md); see [IMPLEMENTATION_PROGRESS.md](IMPLEMENTATION_PROGRESS.md) for current progress.

The original desktop/mobile observations below do not verify the new screens. Final responsive and workflow verification remains pending, and no further compilation or testing is being performed at the user's request.

## Assessment

The catalog has a useful foundation. Keep the recognizable Items, Assemblies, and Storage pages. The largest improvement would be to organize operational work around a specific event and its next outstanding tasks.

Procurement and event usage need changes to their underlying meaning as well as their presentation. In particular, historical “usage” currently falls when equipment is returned, event plans do not feed the procurement calculation, and the purchasing workflow has no goods-receipt screen. Changing cards or colors will not resolve these problems.

Orders have the strongest operational workflow, especially faction orders: preparation, reservations, serialized assets, pickup locations, QR codes, component returns, and history are valuable. Their main problems are inconsistent general/faction behavior, excessive information before the working list, and status labels that overstate progress.

Personal storage is not the same as private ownership or restricted visibility. An organization-owned generator can be kept at a member's home; privately owned equipment can be stored in an organization-managed shed. The interface needs to communicate those distinctions without requiring separate catalogs.

## Scope and evidence

- Reviewed route composition, navigation, theme, all route-level page families, their principal forms/lists, and the backend calculations supporting procurement and event usage.
- Inspected the running local development UI with its existing development data: procurement, event overview, faction order overview/detail, and Items. Reviewed mobile at **390 × 844** and desktop, including **1440 × 1000** for Events. This was not a production-data audit.
- Other screens and role differences below were evaluated from source, not from a complete interactive test of every role and state. No production transaction, purchase, return, or permission change was performed.
- Graphify provided initial navigation; its existing graph warned about an older node-ID format. Findings below were checked against current source rather than treated as facts merely because they appeared in the graph.
- No application implementation was changed during the original audit. Subsequent implementation is recorded separately above. This audit is not an accessibility certification or a complete backend correctness review.

**Priority:** Now = misleading information, missing essential workflow, or serious everyday friction; Next = substantial simplification; Later = refinement. “Confirmed” means directly supported by source or observed UI. “Proposal” means a product decision recommended for this use case.

### Proposed procurement interaction

1. Open procurement within **TNO · 8–11 October**. See “8 item types need sourcing; 3 deliveries expected; 1 late” (illustrative numbers).
2. Open a shortage: “Need 20 radios; 12 usable and committed to this event; 4 expected before setup; 4 uncovered.” Inspect where these quantities came from.
3. Choose an authorized source: move stock from another location, obtain a member's commitment, or add four radios to a supplier purchase.
4. Record the supplier purchase once with all its lines and delivery destination.
5. Warehouse crew receive it. Only accepted receipt updates physical stock; the event's coverage refreshes afterward.

For equipment stored at home, distinguish “exists at Alex's home” from “Alex has agreed to bring it by Thursday.” Do not treat private or unconfirmed equipment as automatically available to the event.

## Recommended event workspace

The selected event should answer four questions without requiring the user to reconstruct the answer from different pages:

| View | Main question | Default content / action |
|---|---|---|
| Overview | Are we ready, and what needs attention? | Missing requests, shortage lines, late deliveries, orders ready, outstanding returns. Each count links to the relevant filtered work. |
| Equipment | What do we need, and where will it come from? | Item/assembly, required amount, committed stock, storage/keeper, expected delivery, uncovered amount. Expand for source requests and stock arithmetic. |
| Orders | What does each team need next? | Requests grouped/filtered by requester and workflow stage; clear next action. Warehouse users can switch to location-based packing. |
| Results | What was issued, consumed, returned, or lost? | Stable historical issue/consumption measures and unresolved custody. Optional comparison with a chosen past event. |

Procurement remains a cross-event workspace for planners, entered from an event shortage with scope preserved. Storage remains a reusable inventory view. Neither has to become a duplicate event dashboard.

## Delivery sequence

The modular-monolith, server-authoritative stock, immutable history, outbox invalidation, and offline idempotency architecture remain appropriate foundations. They do not require the UI to mirror database entities. Introduce focused read models for event coverage and outstanding custody behind the existing service boundaries, while retaining transactional commands for purchase receipt, preparation, handover, and acknowledgement.

The requirements' “Implemented” labels describe capability coverage, not proof that each end-user journey is coherent. In particular, review the relationship between CAT-05 (visibility), LOC-01 (places), PRC-01–03 (deficits/purchases/in-transit), and RET-01–02 (drop-off/acknowledgement). Ownership/keeper commitments and time-aware coverage are product proposals in this audit, not claims that those precise requirements already exist.

1. **Correct meaning and misleading states first:** E1–E4, E8, P1–P5, P14, O2/O5/O8, C2, R2/R3, N7. Add authoritative event/custody projections where required; do not reproduce ledger arithmetic in each React page.
2. **Build the event and sourcing journey:** shared occurrence context, equipment coverage, multi-line purchase recording, and the warehouse receiving path. Decide how plans, requests, private contributions, and time-dependent stock interact before polishing these views.
3. **Simplify operational screens:** one next action, compact mobile rows, shared request shell, coherent return queue, searchable filters, collapsed history. Preserve the catalog and existing faction-order mechanics.
4. **Finish consistency and resilience:** contrast, localization, role-specific actions, keyboard/touch behavior, cached/pending state, dirty-form handling, and export layout.

Avoid treating all “Now” findings as one giant redesign. The first release can correct event usage, expose planned lines, preserve notes, fix misleading labels/states, and open read-only detail navigation independently of the larger sourcing workflow.

## Acceptance scenarios for implementation

These are proposed checks, not tests executed during this audit.

1. Issue ten radios and accept ten back: historical issued stays ten, still-out becomes zero, and the next-event baseline remains useful.
2. Plan twenty radios before any handover: all planned lines appear, with provisional/confirmed need clearly distinguished.
3. Add a general request and a faction request: both contribute under a documented demand rule, without duplicating the event target.
4. Two sequential events can reuse the same returnable stock; overlapping commitments expose a conflict. A receipt arriving after setup does not cover an earlier shortage.
5. A supplier purchase of three item types is recorded once; partial receipt creates only accepted physical stock at the selected destination.
6. Organization-owned equipment stored at a member's home remains distinguishable from private equipment and central warehouse stock; a private contribution requires an explicit commitment before being treated as covered.
7. A user with catalog-view permission can open item/assembly details but cannot edit them. Warehouse crew can receive goods without procurement-planning access. User administration is offered only to administrators.
8. An old submitted faction request does not mark the current occurrence's request complete. “Ready” consistently communicates whether stock is reserved.
9. Consumed/written-off quantities no longer appear as ordinary items still to return. Each serialized asset retains its identity. A pending drop-off stays unavailable until accepted.
10. Scanning returns starts from a deliberate count/inspection state. “Everything returned intact” is an explicit action with a clear quantity summary.
11. At 390 × 844, the event/order/shortage page shows useful working records without first scrolling through a full screen of summaries; primary actions do not require horizontal scrolling. Validate 320px, tablet, desktop, text zoom, and both languages/themes too.
12. Failed queries never produce “No shortages” or “No maintenance due.” Closing/refetching an editor cannot silently discard edits. Saving dates preserves existing notes.
13. Queued offline operations remain visibly pending until acknowledged; conflicts identify the affected item/order and provide a clear recovery route. Online-only operations explain that requirement before the user completes a form.
14. Long item names, large histories, empty states, slow connections, partial lists, late deliveries, and unavailable suppliers remain usable. PDF exports maintain readable row heights and repeated headers.
